The biggest mistake people make after a painful experience is trying to get past it as quickly as possible. Move on, stay busy, don't think about it, that sounds healthy, but it isn't. I spent years treating suffering as a problem to solve, to get through it, to put it behind me, to get back to normal, and I kept noticing in my own life and in the research that the people who did that actually didn't heal faster. They just stopped feeling, which is not the same thing. We're wired to avoid pain because pain signals danger. That was useful for survival. It's much less useful for building a meaningful life because some of the most important things that will ever happen to you will hurt, and if you rush past the lesson, you lose what it was trying to show you. The Buddhists have understood this for two and a half thousand years. Modern psychology is catching up. Researchers who study post -traumatic growth keep finding the same pattern. People who sit with their suffering, who ask what is teaching them, rather than how to escape it, often come out with something the comfortable life ever gave them. Sharper priorities, deeper relationships, a clearer sense of what actually matters. The practice I give my students is simple. When something painful happens, don't ask, how do I get over this? Ask instead, what is this trying to teach me? That's a different project, and it's a harder one, but it's the one that leads somewhere. That question, what is this trying to show me, is what the meaning membership is built around. Not to arrive at easy answers, but to become the kind of people who can hold the real answers. Because meaning isn't something you find once, it's something that accumulates, and it accumulates fastest when you stop running from the things that are trying to teach you.
